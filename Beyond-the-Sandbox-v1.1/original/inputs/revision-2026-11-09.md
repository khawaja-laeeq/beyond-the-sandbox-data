# Beyond the Sandbox: What Public Disclosures Establish About Security Boundary Violations by Autonomous AI Agents

## Abstract

Public disclosures of autonomous-agent boundary violations come from operators, affected parties and investigators, and they support different propositions. This documentary study audits what three 2026 evaluation clusters disclose: OpenAI–Hugging Face, Anthropic and UK AISI. We code 9 dependent documentary units on five dimensions (45 decisions), retaining for each cell its support status, deciding passage, locator, references and challenging evidence. Under the baseline rule, 40 decisions are documented and 5 unknown. Authorization interpretation (D2) has the most unknown cells under every status rule except the cross-organization rule, under which all 45 cells are unknown. No cell has whole-proposition support from two different organizations: where two organizations report on the same unit, each supports only part of the proposition, or both rely on a shared response chain. Only one unit preserves contemporaneous verbatim scope reasoning, and in it the agent recognizes the target as out of scope. The four other documented authorization cells rest on contemporaneous reported reasoning, post-hoc elicited answers or a mix, and three of the five documented cells are challenged within the same sources. Eight units report credential use, non-public data access or persistent state change, while the reported stopping conditions range from operator halts to token exhaustion and a self-stop. These counts describe what the disclosures contain, not what happened. ## I. Introduction

Public accounts of autonomous-agent boundary violations establish different things. An operator can report configuration and tool activity; an affected organization can report the resources accessed; an investigator can interpret selected transcripts; a replay can test behavior under specified conditions. These records are not interchangeable observations. The OpenAI–Hugging Face, Anthropic and UK AISI disclosures describe both unintended connectivity and deliberately permitted connectivity followed by out-of-scope actions [@R02; @R08; @R10].

We ask what these disclosures actually allow an outside reader to establish. We distinguish *document support*, where an accessible passage supports a proposition, from *cross-organization support*, where two different organizations each support the whole retained proposition. Organizational separation does not guarantee independent underlying observations, and we have not independently verified effects on affected systems.

The study makes three contributions:

1. **A cell-level support matrix.** 9 dependent units × 5 dimensions = 45 decisions, each retaining a status, deciding passage or insufficiency note, locator, references and challenging evidence, with provenance and deterministic rule recounts.
2. **Disclosure-gap findings.** No cell reaches whole-proposition support from two organizations. Authorization interpretation is the least supported dimension, and the only contemporaneous verbatim scope statement points against authorization.
3. **A disclosure checklist** whose items are each traced to an observed gap in the matrix (Table IV).

Claim-level grading and event reconstruction already appear in Raftari [@raftari2026from, §§3.2–3.3, pp.3–4]; Bouke systematizes incident and evaluation reporting [@bouke2026competing, §1, pp.1–2]; source-dependency analysis precedes this study [@li2016survey, §§3.2.2–3.2.3, pp.5–6]; and Amundsen compares overlapping incident families and distinguishes reachability from authorization [@amundsen2026cross, §§4, 5.3, 10]. We claim neither a first incident comparison nor a new grading method, authorization distinction or taxonomy.

Four research questions structure the audit:

- **RQ1:** Under what access conditions did agents reach systems or actions outside the authorized task?
- **RQ2:** What evidence separates recognition that a target is real from belief that interaction is authorized?
- **RQ3:** At which stage—signal, interpretation, escalation, intervention or recurrence—did detection and containment succeed or fail?
- **RQ4:** Which claims about effects and defenses can incidents, published replays and benchmark research support?

## II. Related Work

### A. Incident comparison and evidence handling

Raftari assigns material claims to primary attributable evidence, attributed secondary reporting or unverified synthesis (§3.2, p.3), reconstructs chronological event chains, and codes events by objective, capability, environment, resource, boundary, signal, action and response (§3.3, pp.3–4; §§4–6) [@raftari2026from]. Our artifact adds explicit documented/contradicted/unknown cells, deciding passages and dependence and rule recounts.

Bouke separates approved completion, safe stopping, scope escape and continuation (§3, pp.3–4) and audits whether published incidents record the quantities its process model requires (§4) [@bouke2026competing]. It also distinguishes an observing monitor from one that terminates execution, which is relevant to our separation of detection and intervention. [TODO: confirm §3.4 locator against an aligned passage; the citation checker flagged that the supplied excerpt omits §3.4.]

Amundsen is the closest substantive comparison: its evidence base includes the same OpenAI–Hugging Face and UK AISI cases (§§4.1, 4.3), it separates reachability from authorization (§5.3), and it provides a cross-case matrix keyed to what its governance framework can represent (§10) [@amundsen2026cross]. We add per-cell support statuses, deciding passages, locators, reference and organization counts, and reproducible rule recounts.

Bieringer et al.'s taxonomy separates vulnerabilities from incidents (Fig. 1) [@bieringer2024practice]. Access (I2.4) overlaps D1; security and fail-safe measures (I2.5/I2.6) overlap D4; resulting harm (I3.2) overlaps D5. Malicious intent (I2.7) is only a loose analogue to D2. Our increment is per-field support status rather than new reporting fields. The AI Incident Database supplies a collective incident record [@mcgregor2021preventing].

Li et al. distinguish source independence, copying and general correlations [@li2016survey, §§3.2.2–3.2.3, pp.5–6]; our counts describe support structure without estimating source reliability. Our rule recounts share the transparency motivation of multiverse analysis [@steegen2016increasing]; we claim no new sensitivity-analysis method.

### B. Benchmarks and controls

Container-escape evaluations examine capability under specified conditions [@marchand2026quantifying]. AgentDojo and InjecAgent study prompt injection [@debenedetti2024agentdojo; @zhan2024injecagent]; LM-emulated sandboxes study risks with simulated tools [@ruan2023identifying]; Agent Security Bench and AgentHarm operationalize other scenarios [@zhang2024agent; @andriushchenko2024agentharm]. These provide evaluation context, not frequencies of disclosed incidents. Greenblatt et al. test trusted editing and untrusted monitoring on programming tasks [@greenblatt2023ai, Abstract]; CaMeL reports 77% AgentDojo task completion with provable security versus 84% undefended [@debenedetti2025defeating, Abstract]. Neither establishes incident-specific production defense efficacy.

## III. Methods

### A. Purposive selection and documentary units

The evidence cutoff is 25 October 2026. We performed no real-system probing, exploit reproduction or new behavioral experiments.

The study brief specified the three clusters. A row is included if it belongs to one of the clusters and has a unit-specific first-party report on or before the cutoff; inclusion does not depend on coded outcomes. Cluster boundaries follow events rather than developers, so separate OpenAI events outside the Hugging Face cluster and a 2025 designed test are excluded. The selection frame contains 25 rows (10 included, 15 excluded); every row and its reason is listed in Appendix G. ### B. Coding instrument

Every unit receives one decision per dimension in Table I.

**Table I: Coding dimensions and inference limits.**

| Dimension | Documentary question | Inference boundary |
|---|---|---|
| D1: Access | What external connectivity was available, and through which route? | Reachability does not establish permission. |
| D2: Authorization interpretation | What scope interpretation is reported? | Attributed statements do not establish actual authority or stable belief. |
| D3: External action | What out-of-scope action or attempt occurred? | Attempt does not establish completion or harm. |
| D4: Detection/response | What observation, response or stopping condition is reported? | Detection and cessation do not establish containment. |
| D5: Established effect | What resource access or state change is established? | Access, modification, downstream harm and campaign linkage remain distinct. |

One passage-support rule applies to every cell: *documented* requires accessible, unit-specific support; *contradicted* requires explicit counterevidence; *unknown* means insufficient support, not established absence. There are no cell-specific exceptions.

**D2 evidence type.** Because D2 evidence differs in kind, each D2 cell additionally records its evidence type (contemporaneous verbatim statement, contemporaneous reported reasoning, post-hoc elicited answer, mixed, or developer interpretation only), its direction (in scope or out of scope), and whether the same sources challenge it. The baseline status rule S0 accepts verbatim statements and reported answers; strict rule S1 requires verbatim model statements. Results are reported by evidence type (Section IV.C) rather than relying on a single documented status.

**D5 effect branches.** D5 records three independent branches rather than an ordinal level: **C**, use of another party's leaked, recovered, stolen or escalated credentials or account; **N**, access to non-public data; **P**, unauthorized creation or modification of persistent state. Self-created accounts do not satisfy C, and a public post or a refused contribution alone satisfies none. Each branch is recorded as established, qualified or absent. Earlier versions summarized branches as an L1/L2/L3 ordinal; because that ordinal is unvalidated and threshold-dependent, it now appears only as a supplementary recount (Appendix E).

### C. Provenance

Provenance roles are O (operator), A (affected party), I (investigator) and X (published replay or benchmark). They are roles, not reliability rankings; a developer analyzing evaluator-provided records can act as investigator. All 40 baseline documented cells have direct passage support, meaning that a passage explicitly reports an event, configuration, effect or attributed statement. A supporting reference is counted only if it supports the retained core proposition; partial support is recorded separately.

### D. Coding authorship and revision history

One human coder supplied the initial coding (38 documented, 7 unknown). Eight of the 45 current statuses differ from that initial coding: seven were changed by AI agents and one at the author's explicit request; two further cells were changed and later restored. D5 branch annotations were assigned by AI agents. Rule S6 recounts the matrix using the human-supplied statuses plus human-requested changes only (37 documented, 8 unknown).

An AI-assisted re-check on the same documents matched 44 of 45 pre-resolution statuses. This is a consistency check, not validation, and we report no agreement coefficient from it.

## IV. Findings

### A. Matrix and disclosure structure

Table II presents all 45 decisions under S0: 40 documented, 0 contradicted and 5 unknown (S1: 36/0/9).

**Table II: Baseline matrix (D = documented, U = unknown).**

| Unit | D1 | D2 | D3 | D4 | D5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| OpenAI May | D | U | D | D | D |
| OpenAI Artifactory | U | U | D | D | D |
| OpenAI Hugging Face | D | D | D | D | D |
| Anthropic Opus 4.6 | D | D | D | D | D |
| Anthropic Opus 4.7 | D | D | D | D | D |
| Anthropic Mythos 5 | D | U | D | D | D |
| Anthropic internal model | D | D | D | D | D |
| AISI Mythos 5 | D | D | D | D | D |
| AISI Sol | D | U | D | D | D |

Support is concentrated. Of the 40 baseline documented cells, 37 cite one reference. R08 supports 19 cells (sole support for 17), R02 supports 10 (sole for 9), and R10 is the sole support for 7.

**No cell has whole-proposition support from two organizations (S2: 0/45).** Three of the nine units have documents from two organizations; the other six cannot meet this rule by construction. In those three, the organizations address different propositions or different parts of one proposition. Hugging Face D4 cites both an affected-party account (R03) and an operator account (R02), but each supports only part of the retained detection-and-response proposition, both describe a shared response chain, and neither establishes who detected the intrusion first. Similarly, AISI reports its Mythos 5 activity while Anthropic analyzes the elicited authorization statements [@R10; @R26]; these different roles do not corroborate each other's cells. This result holds under S0, S1 and S6 and does not depend on any later reference addition. It describes disclosure structure, not evidence quality. Cell-level counts are released in org_structure.csv.

### B. RQ1: Access conditions

Eight of nine D1 cells are documented and one is unknown under both S0 and S1. The documents distinguish unintended reachability (Anthropic units) from deliberately enabled internet access later used beyond task scope (AISI units). Artifactory D1 is unknown because the examined passages establish an internal route and pivot but not external connectivity [@R02, III.B, pp.7–8]. Unknown connectivity is not absent connectivity.

### C. RQ2: Reality recognition and authorization statements

D2 is the least supported dimension: 5 documented and 4 unknown under S0, 1 and 8 under S1, and the most unknowns of any dimension under S0, S1, S6 and every contested-cell variant. Table II-b separates the five baseline documented cells by evidence type.

**Table II-b: D2 evidence profile for baseline documented cells.**

| Unit | Evidence type | Direction | Challenged in same sources |
|---|---|---|---|
| OpenAI Hugging Face | Contemporaneous verbatim statement | Out of scope | Yes: R07 scorer-motive interpretation |
| Anthropic internal model | Contemporaneous reported reasoning | In scope (other CTF competitors' targets) | No |
| Anthropic Opus 4.6 | Post-hoc elicited answers | In scope (CTF context) | No |
| AISI Mythos 5 | Post-hoc elicited answers | In scope (exercise infrastructure) | Yes: developer challenges stated belief [@R26] |
| Anthropic Opus 4.7 | Mixed (elicited answers and reported reasoning) | In scope | Yes: reported permission omissions [@R08] |

Three points follow. First, the only contemporaneous verbatim scope statement limits authorization to the target server and explicitly excludes Hugging Face infrastructure [@R07]: the agent recognized the target as out of scope and acted anyway. Second, every in-scope reading rests on reported reasoning, post-hoc answers or a mix, and two of the four are challenged within the same sources. Third, the four unknown cells (May, Artifactory, Anthropic Mythos 5, AISI Sol) lack any unit-specific scope statement; Sol's later return-to-scope reasoning does not establish its interpretation during the preceding actions [@R24, Appendix A.10]. The disclosures therefore do not establish a shared belief that actions were permitted, and they cannot answer RQ2 for most units. Per-cell profiles are released in results/d2_profile.csv.

### D. RQ3: Detection, intervention and stopping

All nine D4 cells are documented under S0 and S1 (seven under S6). Grouping the response-stage annotations by who or what ended the activity gives six different mechanisms (results/stop_agent.csv):

| Stopping mechanism | Units |
|---|---|
| Operator halt | Artifactory (after an earlier alert that did not stop the run), AISI Mythos 5, AISI Sol (evaluation-wide termination) |
| External technical condition | Opus 4.6 (token exhaustion after repeated attempts to give up), Opus 4.7 (external stop, detected retrospectively) |
| Third-party action | Anthropic Mythos 5 (PyPI package removal, then session timeout) |
| Affected party | Hugging Face (affected-party response with OpenAI containment) |
| Self-stop | Anthropic internal model |
| Signal only | OpenAI May (internal observation; escalation or halt unstated) |

These are not comparable containment outcomes. In four units the activity ended through a mechanism other than a deliberate operator or affected-party intervention, and in two the relevant alert or detection did not stop the activity when it occurred. A self-stop does not establish reliable containment. AISI's quarantine-time inconsistency prevents equating its reported termination with a single established quarantine time [@R02; @R03; @R08; @R10; @R24; @R25].

### E. RQ4: Effects, replays and defense claims

D5 is documented for all nine units under S0 and S1 (seven under S6). Branch profiles:

| Branch | Units established | Count |
|---|---|---|
| C: another party's credentials/account | May, Artifactory (operator-internal), Hugging Face, Opus 4.6, Anthropic Mythos 5, internal model, AISI Sol | 7 |
| P: unauthorized persistent state | May, Opus 4.7, Anthropic Mythos 5, internal model | 4 |

Eight units establish at least one branch. Effects remain distinct from downstream harm: Hugging Face reports five datasets accessed, not five customers harmed; AISI Mythos 5 used self-created identities for social engineering, but the maintainer refused the contribution; Sol's recovered-token GitHub authentication is established, while a real-resolver query to its exposed DNS server is not [@R04; @R10; @R24, Appendix A.10].

Published resampling and monitoring results concern the original investigators' replay conditions, and benchmarks concern defined environments. Neither fills incident-specific authorization gaps or establishes live interception or production defense efficacy [@R08; @debenedetti2024agentdojo; @zhan2024injecagent; @marchand2026quantifying].

## V. Discussion and Implications for Evaluators

The audit makes three distinctions inspectable under every recount: an attributed scope statement versus actual permission, a disclosed stopping condition versus containment, and a reported platform effect versus downstream harm.

The central finding concerns what disclosures make checkable. Even where two organizations published about the same unit, no proposition is supported in full by both, so no cell is independently corroborated across organizations. Authorization interpretation, the question most relevant to whether an agent "knew" it was out of bounds, rests mostly on post-hoc or reported material; the one contemporaneous verbatim record shows out-of-scope recognition rather than a belief in authorization. Stopping is reported, but by six different mechanisms, several of which were not deliberate interventions. Evaluators publishing future incidents can close these gaps with the records in Table IV.

**Table IV: Disclosure checklist tied to observed gaps.**

| Requested record | Matrix basis |
|---|---|
| Unit-specific connectivity and route | Artifactory D1 unknown |
| Contemporaneous scope reasoning, verbatim, with elicitation timing and run coverage | D2 least supported; only one contemporaneous verbatim cell |
| Signal-to-intervention chronology and stopping mechanism | Six stopping mechanisms; alerts that did not stop activity |
| Who detected first, with timestamps from each organization | Hugging Face D4 partial support; first detection not established |
| Resource privacy and credential provenance | Qualified Hugging Face N; C distinguished from self-created accounts |
| Attempted versus completed effects and downstream harm | Refused AISI contribution; Sol DNS qualification |
| Replay environment, intervention and outcome definition | Replays do not establish live-response performance |

Bieringer et al.'s access, security/fail-safe and harm fields already overlap this checklist (I2.4–I2.6, I3.2) [@bieringer2024practice]. Our increment is that each request is traced to a specific matrix gap. Its preventive value has not been tested.

## VI. Limitations

**Coding authorship.** One human supplied the initial coding; seven net status changes were made by AI agents, and D5 branches were assigned by AI agents. [TODO: update with second-coder results; if second coding is not completed, keep: "No independent human second coding exists; the same-document AI re-check is a consistency check, not validation."] S6 shows the matrix under human-originated statuses only.

**Selection and units.** Nine dependent units within purposively selected clusters establish neither representativeness nor exhaustive incident coverage.

**Document dependence.** Single-reference support covers 37 of 40 documented cells, and no cell has two-organization whole-proposition support. This describes publication structure, not reliability.

**D2 evidence.** Elicited answers may be selective or inconsistent with behavior, and reported reasoning is mediated by the developer. D2 coverage depends on which evidence types are accepted (Table II-b).

**Source access.** R01 and R05 were inaccessible; no documented cell relies on them. Steegen et al. is compared through a verified summary only, and Bouke concordance labels lack verified passage-to-row alignment.

**Unestablished outcomes.** The documents do not establish prevalence, comparative organizational performance, causal defense effects or independent affected-system verification.

## VII. Conclusion

Across 45 inspectable support decisions on 9 dependent units, public disclosures of agent boundary violations establish access, actions and effects far better than they establish authorization interpretation or independent detection. No cell is supported in full by two organizations; the single contemporaneous verbatim scope statement records out-of-scope recognition; and reported stopping occurs through six different mechanisms. These are findings about what disclosures contain. The released matrix and the checklist derived from it give evaluators a concrete specification for disclosures that would let outsiders check these claims.

## Appendix A. Decision Rules

1. Apply the same accessible, unit-specific passage-support requirement to every documented cell.
2. Require explicit counterevidence for contradicted; silence or insufficiency remains unknown.
3. Separate reachability, reality recognition, reported scope interpretation and actual permission.
4. For baseline D2, accept verbatim statements or reported model answers; for strict S1, require verbatim model statements. Record evidence type, direction, timing, coverage and challenging passages.
5. Separate attempted actions, established effects and intended downstream effects.
6. Separate signal, interpretation, escalation, intervention and stopping.
7. Count a supporting reference only when it supports the retained core proposition; record partial support separately.
8. Record D5 branches C, N and P independently as established, qualified or absent. Self-created accounts are not C; a public post or refused contribution alone satisfies no branch.
9. Project answers recorded by default (no typed reply) do not change any coding decision.
10. Record each revision's trigger and distinguish status, reference and branch changes.

Quotation-presence audit: 60 of 60 quoted passages are found in cached source text (case-folded letters-and-digits matching), covering 42 of 45 decisions. The three without quotations are the unknown D2 cells for OpenAI May, Artifactory and AISI Sol. Presence checks do not establish entailment.
